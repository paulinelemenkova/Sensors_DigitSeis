\abstract{
Archived seismograms recorded in XX century present valuable source of information for monitoring earthquake activity and geophysical modelling. However, old data are only available as scanned paper-based images that should be digitised and converted from raster to vector format. Seismograms have special characteristics recorded by seismometer and encrypted in the images: traces, minute time gaps, timing and wave amplitudes. This information should be recognised and interpreted. Our objective was to automatically digitise historical seismograms recorded by Galitzine seismometer in 1954 in Uccle seismic station, Belgium. A dataset included 145 TIFF images. Software for digitising seismograms are limited and many have disadvantages. We used DigitSeis and performed a full workflow which included pattern recognition, classification, digitising, corrections and converting TIFFs to digital output. Selected images were damaged with identified defects (spots, overlaps of traces). Such files were corrected interactively. The generated contours of signals were presented as time series and converted into digital MATLAB format (.mat files) which preserved the ground motion information contained in analog seismograms. The DigitSeis approach demonstrated a powerful and reliable toolset for processing analog seismic signals. This study contributed to methodological development of signal processing for seismograms archived in Royal Observatory of Belgium.
}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}
The seismicity of the Earth represent a physical phenomena resulting from inner tectonic processes connected to energy accumulation and release \cite{GutenbergRichter}. The fundamental concepts of the Earth's physics are reflected in the movements on the surface that have different intensity of the vibrations, caused by the fluctuations of energy \cite{Shearer2019}. In the geodynamic sense, seismicity is the result of self-organisation of the Earth, responding to the internal processes of the lithosphere movements by the released energy \cite{UdiasBuforn2017}. Measuring seismic signals of the Earth by seismometers enables to evaluate ground noises and shaking of the Earth’s interior, associated with earthquakes of various magnitude. 

The importance of the seismogram datasets can be illustrated by their practical application in relation to tectonics, geology and physics of the Earth. Specifically, the wide use of seismograms includes such fundamental domains as geophysics \cite{Kulhanek}, subsurface exploration, global and planetary tectonics \citep{Crampin,Jackson2002}, risk assessment in civil engineering \citep{Pileckietal2017, Kurzeja}, prediction of earthquakes and relative analysis (dynamic rupture, seismic tomography and wave forms) \cite{Amorese,Lomnitz}, volcanology \cite{Pezzo,DePlaenetal2018,DePlaenetal2018b}. Analysis of seismograms is based on the interpreting of observation datasets to quantify seismic sources: location, size, rupture history, travel time and wave polarization \cite{Ammon2021}. Seismic data processing involves a variety of approximation methods, e.g. Fourier analysis, or an \ac{AI} modelling of the synthetic generated data \cite{Rosenbaum1994}.

For instance, earthquake-related hazards can be simulated by artificial Fortran-based stochastic time series of faults \cite{BeresnevAtkinson}. The evaluation of this method was further extended by \cite{NicknamEslamian} who generated artificial seismograms for modelling seismotectonic processes. These include, for instance, such aspects as fault length and width, analysis of sensitivity with varied epicenter locations and rupture processes showing the extent of slip of the Earth's crust, e.g. rupture velocity and shear wave velocity. Synthetic seismograms can furthermore point at the geological processes by analysis of seismic reflectors of stratigraphic data \cite{BrewMayer1998}. Besides geological domains, seismograms can be used in environmental studies to reveal the characteristics of seismic storms and their correlation with climate change and dynamic processes of the Earth \cite{Singh2021,Toyokunietal2015}.

Predictive modelling of seismograms for analysis of earthquake hazards is largely based on the observation seismology and retrieval of information from the digital seismograms \cite{Claerbout1992}. Uniformed, standardised, converted to digital format and accurately processed seismograms enable to explain the physical nature and repeatability of earthquakes based on the ground motion signals. However, the use of such information relies on our access to the large datasets of seismograms. Generating such data pools is only possible by accurate and standardised recording system using commonly accepted and approved instruments (seismometers and seismographers) that continues through years and centuries and regularly provides data for further processing \cite{UdiasBuforn2017}. 

\subsection{Historical development}
The history of seismography undergone a long process of technical evolution of the \ac{EO} data processing\cite{DeweyByerly1979,Agnew}. In general, the instruments used for seismic data recording reflect a constant development of technologies and a state-of-the-art in methods, which provide tools to measure ground motion, earthquake's magnitude, location and frequency \cite{DeweyByerly1979}. The systematic and precise operative observation of seismicity of the Earth began in the latter part of the \nth{19} century along with a progress in technology, development of new seismographs, and general advances in geophysical methods \cite{SchweitzerLee2003}. At this time, since 1880s seismologists started recording continuously and systematically the ground motion using seismographs \cite{SchweitzerLee2003}. From that period and up to 1960s the seismograms were recorded as analogue signals using technical abilities of the narrow-band, low-range instruments of those time \cite{Batlloetal2008}.

During the first half of the \nth{20} century seismograms continued to be recorded in paper analog format \cite{Wangetal2014} or as magnetic tapes \cite{Anetal2015}. Starting with 1960s, the progress in seismography continued along with the onset of computer era, development of microprocessors and algorithms of geophysical data processing. Apart from advances in instrumental development, seismic data increased in quantity as systematically collected by seismic stations worldwide \cite{Engdahl2002}. An unprecedented initiative of global seismological network \ac{WWSSN} was created in 1960s and generated a collection of high-quality big seismic data \citep{OliverMurphy,Ammon2010}. The initiative of seismic datasets of \ac{WWSSN} network was possible due to the uniformity: standardised technical equipment and workflow for data calibration in each station.

Currently, seismograms are recorded and stored digitally in high-performance data centres using advanced algorithms of computation seismology \cite{Heiner}. The progress in computational seismology now enables numerical approximations of the seismic waves for modelling signals as complex 3D structures, using advanced mathematical solutions, such as Fourier series, Chebyshev pseudospectral methods, approximation of traces by Lagrange and Chebyshev polynomials \cite{Heiner}. However, the remaining historical datasets from previous periods form a valuable database containing archived data on seismicity of the Earth that should be used for practical scientific purposes \cite{Ambraseys1983}. 

\subsection{Motivation}
The importance of the historical datasets for geophysical research has been discussed in relevant works \cite{Vogtetal1985, TevesCosta1999,Bono2007,Okal}. Old seismograms present a key source of information regarding the seismicity of the Earth's interior up to the Middle Ages \cite{Alexandre1990a,Alexandre1985,Alexandre1990b}, which can be used for geophysical reconstructions  and past climate modelling. Analysis of seismic velocity as time series analysis, by autocorrelation and noise cross-correlation can be used for monitoring volcanoes \cite{DePlaen2016,Basowetal,DePlaen2019}. Moreover, retrospective analysis of the old seismograms enables to model earthquake cycle as a continuous process and to get insights into the physics of the earthquakes. Finally, interpretation of the old seismograms provides information for the long-term prognosis. Predictive modelling of earthquakes is possible using statistical analysis of historical datasets on earthquakes's locations and magnitude \cite{Kanamori1988}.

\subsection{Objective and goals}
Our study contributes to the existing publications on seismic signal processing with a case of archive seismograms in Belgium. The goal was to perform automated digitisation of seismic traces contained in historical seismograms recorded by the Galitzine seismometer. To achieve this goal, the technical objective was to apply the DigitSeis software and to use its technical functionality for accurate semi-automated vectorising of seismograms. The aim was to convert the existing large historical archive of seismograms (over 140 scanned raster images) from TIFF format into a digital MATLAB output (generate .mat format files) using advanced technical tools of machine processing and minimised handmade work. We needed to do this as automatically as possible. In this way we aimed to revitalise the historical dataset containing ground motion of the Earth in old seismograms recorded in Uccle station 1954.

\subsection{Approaches}
The first attempts to digitise the analogue recordings of seismic events into numeric format started in 1960s. An \emph{ad hoc} algorithm of thinning the lines on scanned seismograms and converting the image into a 0-1 digital matrix was developed by \cite{Jarosch1975}. Another study used a device initially used for weather maps and adjusted for automatic converting of seismic signals into a digital form, and plotting them using a cathode-ray tube display \cite{AdamsAllen1961}. The progress of digitising signals accelerated in 1980s \citep{AspinallLatchman,CrouseMatuschka1983} and 1990s \citep{NorlundGriffiths1993,McGee1995, Harjesetal1993} along with increase of computer-based algorithms and advanced geophysical software. Digitising approaches has become more and more automated since 2000s, along with a rapid development of programming languages and IT tools \cite{Bietal2016,Ishiietal2015,Schenke,Wangetal2014}. 
 
In recent years, the use of automated digitisers of seismograms or accelerograms (recording the acceleration of the strong motion ground) instead of manual vectorisers has received attention for the effective data processing. These include automated recognition of noise \cite{CrouseMatuschka1983}, handling smooth and wigged traces using algorithms for tracing waves based on the pixels context \cite{Bietal2016}, synchronisation of time scale for the three motion components, handling scratches, distortions or line crossings in the image, adjusting rotated position \cite{Trifunacetal1999}, to mention a few. Other problems are caused by the recording methods of the old mechanical seismographs. For instance, these include a high velocity of stylus that does not affect the paper, clipping of amplitudes, low-contrast dark background on a smoked paper, damaged records due to the bad storage conditions \cite{Baskoutasetal2000}. Digitising damaged seismograms can be a not straightforward task.

As a response to the arising needs for automatic, quick and accurate digitising and modelling of seismic data, various programs were developed. The advanced in signal recognition are supported by rapid growth of the programming languages \cite{Esmaili,analytica2030006,Lemenkova201991,Lemenkova2019100,Lemenkov202103}, and specifically, Python \cite{Cox,Stoneback, Lemenkova201989}, improvements of algorithms of \ac{ML} and \ac{DL} for image segmentation \cite{signals1010005,DebeirDecaestecker,signals2030037,Debeir2008}, detecting noise in signal processing \cite{Foucart,Debeiretal2019}. Software for digitising seismograms is based on pattern recognition. For example, the Teseo \cite{Pintoreetal2005} traces raster files using cubic Bézier curves. This vectoriser handles monochrome images by manual and automatic methods and neural networks. A software using C\# uses algorithm of colour scene recognition by Color Scene Filed Method \cite{Wangetal2016}. A program using MATLAB GUI used algorithm of inversion problem for a matrix of model parameters and observed seismic data \cite{XuXu}. Another example of seismic software, the SeisDig by Berkeley Seismological Laboratory presents an interactive MATLAB GUI based tool performing trace inspection, checking time conservation, recognising events and inputing metadata header \cite{BromirskiChuang2003}. An approach to the seismic recording is presented in DigiSeis \cite{Khanetal2012} aimed at digitizing signals using a built-in PC sound card for signal processing. It visualises the two channel seismic data in time and frequency, and enables to perform a time series analysis. Extracting wave data from paper seismograms was attempted by \cite{Liuetal2001} with a seismogram digitization and DB management system using Delphi3 based on syntax of Pascal language.

\subsection{Fundamentals}
The Fourier series is a conceptual theoretical background to seismic signal data analysis. This is because seismic waves have oscillations of amplitudes periodically varying in time. This results in different frequency of wave signals. Such fundamental characteristics of seismic waves are caused by the dispersion and attenuation of signal strength according to the physics of wave excitation and propagation \cite{Ammon305}. 

Seismic waves can be numerically expressed as a superposition of harmonics of a certain frequency (wave number) and amplitude of oscillations \cite{Lowrie,Folland}. The simplest harmonic oscillation of seismic waves has a sinusoidal form. If the value of \emph{a} changes over time \emph{t}, then it has the formula \cite{Puzyrev} as in Eq. \eqref{eq:1}:

\begin{equation}
	a=a_0 sin2\pi f (t+\tau)=a_o sin(\dfrac{2\pi}{T} t + \varphi_0)
\end{equation}\label{eq:1}

where $a_0$ is maximum oscillation amplitude; \emph{T} is oscillation period; $f=1\div T$ - is wave frequency; $\varphi_0$ is a phase angle, which indicates the lateral time delay in the start of a signal reflection recording \cite{Nanda}. The time shift $\tau$ correlates with $\varphi_0$ by the following relationship \cite{Puzyrev}, Eq. \eqref{eq:2}:   

\begin{equation}
	\tau=\dfrac {\varphi_0 T}{2\pi}
\end{equation}\label{eq:2}

The sum of any number of sinusoidal oscillations of waves of given frequency with random amplitudes and initial phases represent a sinusoidal oscillation of the same frequency. If the phases of wave components are randomly distributed and the amplitudes of all sinusoids are the same $a_0$, then the resulting wave oscillation in average will have an amplitude according to the simple expression \cite{Puzyrev}, Eq. \eqref{eq:3}:  

\begin{equation}
	a_{\sum}=a_0 \sqrt{n}
\end{equation}\label{eq:3}

Seismic trace represents a seismic wave field recorded as signals by a receiver in the exact location of the Earth using \ac{GPS} coordinates. The behaviour of seismic waves can be expressed as a Fourier transform which converts a function of time $x(t)$ into a function of frequency $X(\omega)$, \cite{HoelligHoerner}. The mathematical analysis of seismic trace into its sinusoidal components can be performed by the forward Fourier transform \cite{Bracewelletal2012}, using formula in Eq. \eqref{eq:4}:

\begin{equation}
	X(\omega)=\int_{\infty}^{\infty} x(t) exp(-i\omega t) dt,
\end{equation}\label{eq:4}

where $\omega$ is the Fourier dual representing angular frequency of the time variable \emph{t}. The temporal frequency \emph{f} is related to the angular frequency as $\omega$ by $\omega$ = $2\pi f$ \citep{Champeney}.

Conversely, the synthesis of a seismic trace from the individual sinusoidal components is achieved by the inverse Fourier transform as follows \cite{Bracewell}, Eq. \eqref{eq:5}:
 
\begin{equation}
	x(t)=\int_{\infty}^{\infty} X(\omega) exp(-i\omega t) d\omega,
\end{equation}\label{eq:5}

The development of vectorising algorithms for analog seismogram recordings from the \ac{WWSSN} was largely accelerated using algorithms based on of the Fourier transform techniques and computations. The most well-known application of the \ac{FFT} is a Cooley-Tukey \cite{CooleyTukey} which recursively breaks the \ac{DFT} into smaller \ac{DFT}s. In this way, it decomposes a sequence of signal's values into separate components of different frequencies \cite{Kanasewich}. Such approach significantly facilitated the seismic modelling by time-series analysis of signal sequences. 

The major approach of \ac{FFT} in seismology is that it computes the discrete sequence of seismic wave signals and converts it from time to frequency \cite{Loan}. This is achieved by decomposing a sequence of seismic signals into components of different frequencies \cite{Rader} using the formula in Eq. \eqref{eq:6} for \ac{DFT}, described by \cite{Winograd1978}:

\begin{equation}
	X_k=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n\cdot e^-{\dfrac{2\pi i}{N}\cdot kn} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n \cdot \Bigg( cos(\dfrac{2\pi kn}{N}) - i\cdot sin(\dfrac{2\pi kn}{N}) \Bigg)
\end{equation}\label{eq:6}
where $k=0, ... , N-1$ and $e^{-i2\pi kn/N}$ is a primitive Nth root of 1.
 
Conversely, the \ac{IFT} can be expressed by Eq. \eqref{eq:7} 
 \begin{equation}
	x_n=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X_k\cdot e^-{\dfrac{2\pi i}{N}\cdot kn} = \dfrac{1}{N} \cdot \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X_k\cdot \Bigg( cos(\dfrac{2\pi kn}{N}) + i\cdot sin(\dfrac{2\pi kn}{N}) \Bigg)
\end{equation}\label{eq:7}
where $n=0, ... , N-1$

Any complex oscillation of waves can be represented as a sum of sinusoidal oscillations with different frequencies. In seismography, the most important is processes are wave oscillations that can be represented as a sequence of harmonic components with continuously varying frequencies. A harmonic or sinusoidal wave expresses the propagating wave as a function of time and distance \cite{SteinWysession}. In this way it represents the function $f(x\pm vt)$ showing seismic wave as a function of time and distance, Eq. \eqref{eq:8}:
 
\begin{equation}
	u(x,t)=Ae^{i(\omega t\pm kx)} = A \cdot cos (\omega t \pm kx) + Ai \cdot sin (\omega t \pm kx)
\end{equation}\label{eq:8}

Here the harmonic seismic wave is distinct by its amplitude \emph{A} and two parameters, $\omega$, which is the angular frequency $\omega=2\pi f$ where \emph{f} is a frequency of wave $f=1\div T = \omega\div(2\pi)$ and \emph{k} is the wavenumber, or spatial frequency showing how the oscillation repeats in space. The \emph{k} is $2\pi$ times the number of cycles occurring in a unit distance of the seismic wave \cite{SteinWysession}. Thus, the function of time $F(t)$ is associated with the function of frequency $S(\omega)$, where $\omega = 2\pi f$ is the angular frequency. The function $S(\omega)$ shows a frequency range. The relationship between the $F(t)$ and $S(\omega)$ can be expressed in the Fourier integrals using general formulae of \ac{DFT} (Eq. \ref{eq:6}) and \ac{IFT} (Eq. \ref{eq:7}) as follows, Eq. \ref{eq:9} for the \ac{DFT}:

\begin{equation}
	S(\omega) = \int_{\infty}^{\infty} F(t)e^{-j\omega t} dt
\end{equation}\label{eq:9}
where $e^{j\omega t} = cos\omega t + j\cdot sin\omega t; j = \sqrt{-1}$, and for the \ac{IFT}, Eq. \ref{eq:10}: 

\begin{equation}
	F(t) = \dfrac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \int_{\infty}^{\infty} S(\omega)e^{j\omega t} d\omega
\end{equation}\label{eq:10}

Thus, using mathematical formula of \ac{FFT}, a seismic trace can be converted from time function to a frequency function (Fourier analysis) and vice versa (Fourier synthesis) for the digital signal processing \cite{Ammon2021}. This is specifically used in seismology to detect filters and delete coherent noise and in recording of seismometer instruments. 

\subsection{Instrument}
The seismograms have been received from \ac{UCC} using the Galitzine seismometer, Fig. \ref{fig:2}. This type of seismographs is constructed using a following principle: a coil of wire is fixed to the pendulum which oscillates between the poles driven by the earthquake forces. The movement of coil between a strong magnet generate an electric induction current. The current is then being transferred to a sensitive galvanometer with signals registered photographically on the seismogram \cite{Neumann}. It is the \nth{1} electromagnetic instrument developed by B.B. Galitzine in 1910 (Fig. \ref{fig:2}), designed to record a single component (horizontal or vertical) for earthquakes recording \cite{Wgg}. 

\begin{figure}[H]
      	\centering
         		\includegraphics[width=8.0cm]{Galitzine.jpg}
     \caption{Seismic instrument used for raw data capture in 1954: Horizontal Galitzin seismometer located in Uccle seismic station. Image source: Royal Observatory of Belgium.} \label{fig:2}
\end{figure}

B.B. Galitzine reduced the sensitivity of the system by diverting a part of the current and thus improved the accuracy of the system. The special requirements for standard type of operation of these units include the following: (1) Damping the resistance to the galvanometer (2) Damping the seismometer pendulum through the cumulated effect of a magnetic damping device and the external resistance to the pendulum coil. Thus, the structure of the device is presented in Fig. \ref{fig:2}: the pendulum (at the front), consists of a mass suspended by the two wires. A coil is attached to the arm of the pendulum, which moves between the poles of a magnet (at the back), in which a current is being generated. These conditions are satisfied by the Galitzine system that includes a copper plate, fixed on the rod next to the coil, which oscillates in the field of a second magnet and allows for damping by Foucault current. Due to such effectiveness, the Galitzine seismometer is still being used for seismic measurements of Galitzine type. \cite{ChakrabartyTandon}.

The paper-based seismograms used in this study (Fig. \ref{fig:3}) have been generated from the signals of the Galitzine seismometer and a recording system in Uccle station to ground motions of the Earth in the Brabant Massif. The data were recorded by a system of the electromagnetic Galitzine seismometer, which is characterised by the several parts: an electromagnetic seismometer with a coil oscillating in a magnetic field and generating an electric current by induction; Foucault current for damping; horizontal component (7kg); a tracing device with an optical recording system including a photographic paper and a galvanometer mirror with a mobile frame; and the speed settings (period of 12s). 

The raster images containing the earthquake traces have been stored using standard scanners as TIFF pictures which present a matrix of 2D arrays of pixels where each pixel is represented by a number of grayscale corresponding to that pixel \cite{Poynton}. The images have been stored at a very high resolution, ca. 350-400 MB size each, in 256 B/W monochrome scales. The standard format of all the images ensured the integrity and compatibility of the whole dataset used for analysis. 

The scanned images of old seismograms have different features and characteristics, which increase technical difficulties of processing such data: line breaks, distortions, defects, blurred lines, spots, etc. For instance, some data had defects due to the record imperfectness or storage cases (Fig. \ref{fig:2}). As an illustration, we show the examples of such distortions in subplots of Fig. \ref{fig:2}: (a) shows a seismogram with notable empty records between the lines of traces; (b) shows the partially spotted image caused by storage over time; (c) illustrates the continuous noise dark background on the image with blurred traces which creates lack of contrast for automated image recognition; (d) shows overlapped traces which cause a problem during vectorising. Technical tasks concerned the quality of the recordings on such data with each individual seismogram and challenges to automatically recognise traces on the old scanned paper. 

\subsection{Software and Workflow}
In this study we used the DigitSeis, a MATLAB based software for processing analog scanned seismograms \cite{BogiatzisIshii2016} using a structured workflow, Fig \ref{fig:4}.

The DigitSeis is developed as a software instrument for digitising seismograms obtained by the paper scanning. It applies the extracts information from the scanned seismograms processed as an image matrix. DigitSeis converts scanned raster images of analog seismograms from original TIFF format into the convenient digital format using signal processing algorithms. The approach of DigitSeis is based on the principles of semi-automated algorithms of signal processing as time series. It applies minor human supervision, mostly for adjustment of functionality in selected processes and monitoring technical workflow (Fig. \ref{fig:5}).

An embedded algorithm in the DigitSeis uses trace analyses which enables to track the lines and record their values using time gaps. We applied this method to identify traces on scanned TIFF files of seismograms and processes them by automatic detection of lines and adjustment of the distortions (broken lines, spots, repetitive gaps on the series of lines, etc). The digitising process was adjusted to distinguish the presence or absence of lines, similar to the '0-1' matrix, using methods of colour recognition on the image. However, the directions of vector lines present another task that can only be solved using advanced analysis of the geometric trend of the trace by \ac{ML} tools. The thickness of the traces and time marks of the raw data were considered by the DigitSeis for each 30-min line on the seismograms. The workflow of seismogram vectorisation by DigitSeis consists in several steps that can be summarised in the five major steps as follows and described in details below: 1) Image preprocessing; 2) Identification of time marks and traces; 3) Image vectorising and classification; 4) Trace correction and control; 5) Timing and conversion (SAC).

\subsubsection{Image loading and preprocessing}
First, the images were loaded into the program as TIFF files, converted into the native 8-bit HSV formal of DigitSeis and prepared for further processing. Visually, the image contained white traces of measured seismic signals over a black background (Fig. \ref{fig:3}). In the selected images with limited data and large dark background the areas of interest were cropped to reduce the space without traces and to select only a part of image to test a smaller space area of interest. The resulted analysis was saved continuously throughout the workflow into a in the binary data MATLAB format as a .mat file.

\subsubsection{Identifying time gaps on seismograms}
Afterwards, we evaluated the 1-second time mark dimensions which show the breaks of the line along its course. The whole line takes 30 min intervals on the seismogram which shows the occurrence of the earthquake signals for each line (Fig. \ref{fig:4}). It was performed by measuring width and offset of the gaps in the traces and recording the values in the menu bar. Because the majority of lines show images without time marks but with gaps, the time mark width was set to a negative number (-22), Fig. \ref{fig:5}. 

Identifying time gaps is a preparatory step before classification, because time marks are used to properly identify breaks between the signal traces. In out dataset, the seismograms had only gaps for time marks, without time marks, thus we needed only to identify traces as the only necessary objects. To receive an accurate mean measurement values, we indicated time marks on the raster seismograms as -22 (so the gaps of smaller that this value could be included). After the time mark width was set up, the signals were prepared for classification. After the time marks were defined, the program identified traces based on the analysis of areas of contrast in a monochrome colour scale and recognised the position of the traces. Once we identified the gaps indicating time marks, our seismograms were ready for classification.

\subsubsection{Classification}
 
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.3\textwidth}

The aim of the classification algorithm is to discriminate the traces from the time marks and noises, and to perform a complete object recognition, i.e., all objects are to be classified and assigned to the correct category. The image threshold for classification was set as 82 (Fig. \ref{fig:5} (b)), representing the minimum intensity value of a pixel to be included in classified objects. In our case, 82 was appropriate number for moderately clear images and thin lines. The Classification was performed using 'Classify Objects' tool of DigitSeis and the objects on the image were classified into 2 categories: 'traces' and 'noise' (in our case we did not have time marks, but time gaps instead). In some cases, some noise objects were misclassified into the 'trace' category and vice versa. Therefore, their categories were adjusted accordingly using the 'Change Classification' function. Here traces are the separate segments in a seismogram interrupted by time gaps. These time gaps mean minute intervals in traces, necessary to calculate timing. Noise include all irrelevant objects and annotations on the seismograms, such as spots or handwritten texts and work notations, usually written on the edges of scanned seismograms.

Using 'Classification' tool of DigitSeis we automatically converted the image from raster TIFF file into the vector binary format, and classified scanned image into a set of objects with 2 main categories: 1) traces; 2) noise. As mentioned before, this dataset did not have time marks, but time gaps instead; otherwise we would have a third class of objects -- 'time marks'. The classification was performed by partition of image into classes and assignment of pixels into each categories. The assignment was done by the algorithm of classification which identified lines and traces, and discriminated them from noise (and time marks, if available).

\section{Discussion}

Using scanned seismograms from the available archives to explore the atmosphere-ocean-solid earth interactions has many applications in geosciences to study the past seismicity of the Earth including climate modelling. Archives of old data containing seismograms recorded during the pre-satellite period is a crucial source of information for such models. To fully utilise the potential of historical seismograms, the abilities and advantages of modern digitising systems should be used. In our research we used the functionality of DigitSeis for classification and vectorising several old seismograms from the available dataset collection and converted the data into the .mat files. We have extracted the features of objects automatically detected on seismograms and derived the vector file containing classified objects (segments of line traces, noise and time gaps). 

To do this, we introduced a classification driven approach of DigitSeis for processing analog seismograms. Using the functionality of this programs we presented a workflow where we showed that it performs well on datasets without time marks (we had time gaps instead). The algorithms of DigitSeis processed well relatively large files (each scanned seismogram has a size of ca. 350-400 MB). Some misclassified segments are presented in complex regions of seismograms with overlapped traces where the corrections required manual adjustments.

The process of classifying and digitising paper-based seismograms is based on computing features of the scanned objects (lines and other marks, normally classified as noise). The major principle of DigitSeis is creating the decision on classifying training lines (traces of seismograms) based on threshold test. For instance, we set up experimentally the time gaps from -22 to -12 to indicate the small distance breaking the minute marks, which was used by the machine to identifies all those gaps through out the image. Based on the setup parameters of threshold, the machine identified the objects on seismograms by a step-by-step analysis for the whole image.

This workflow arrived at classification of the image and digitising the objects (converting the output file into the .mat format). However, certain segments of the lines required manual tracing the paths and corrections, as demonstrated previously. The most complicated cases (overlapped traces) were studied separately to gain more insights in the visual characteristics of these segments and to discriminate the traces from noise signals. To do so, we zoomed selected enlarged areas on the seismogram and performed corrections manually using a crosshair. Specifically, we identified broken segments, crossed directions of the lines and overlay in traces. 

For each of those cases we traced the line (a 30-minute trace on each of the seismograms) and searched for correct path of the line and zero-lines. Graphical representations of the encountered problems and complicated cases are illustrated in relevant sections. Our approach to separating traces and noise from the background on the photo paper and to automatically recognise time gaps on the seismograms is based on the algorithms embedded in DigitSeis which consists in screening and examining each pixel of the raster image through the defined classifier thresholds. For example, the parameters for image classification included image threshold 82\%, time mark width for gaps as -12 (in some cases up to -22, depending on the quality of scanned paper) and soon, Fig. \ref{fig13}. 

Examples displaying the discrimination of the traces based on admissible properties using threshold intervals resulted in classified and automatically digitised seismograms, Fig. \ref{fig14}. Our approach to automatically recognise and separate traces on the old seismograms from noise and image background consists of using screening the whole image using threshold parameters. The setup key parameters included image threshold and intervals of time gaps corresponding to the minute marks made by the drum of the seismometer.

We presented the practical application of DigitSeis software for digitising scanned historical seismograms in a semi-automatic regime. We note that while using a good digitiser is a major task of any seismological research, there are still more algorithms that need to be adjusted for a fully automated system of classification and digitising of seismograms. Processing seismograms is an essential part of geophysical research having its specifics compared to simple images: minute time marks made by the drum, seismogram timing, recognition of line directions, smooth data conversion, etc. Our goal was to present here a practical application in signal processing by DigitSeis using dataset on historical seismograms with time gaps.

\section{Conclusions}

Digitising seismograms is a time-consuming and laborious task that is difficult to perform manually. Therefore, advanced solutions and \ac{ML} techniques are useful tools for signal processing. In this paper we presented the processing workflow of digitising seismograms using DigitSeis software and illustrated practical steps performed by its embedded algorithms. The aim of this research was to digitise selected scanned images from a dataset of the historical archives of seismograms recorded by the Galitzine seismometer in 1954 in Uccle station, Belgium.

Geophysics faces enormous challenges in meeting the rising demand for methods of effective data processing, because seismic data grow in volumes, which include both historical and recently recorded seismograms. Earthquakes are the most important types of the world's geological hazards significantly affecting environment, human's lives and property. The analysis of ground motions of the Earth is a background for prognosis of earthquakes, which requires the use of the advanced software for effective processing of seismic data using \ac{ML} approaches of signal recognition and interpretation. Analog historical seismograms are the valuable source of information for retrospective modelling of the Earth's seismicity. 

Old scanned seismograms can be effectively used in geophysical research if properly processed. Manual digitising of these data is usually time-consuming, however, these data should be digitised before they can be further analysed and released in an accessible manner for further geophysical analysis. To meet these challenges and make geophysical research more accurate and thorough, effective approaches in processing analog seismograms are needed urgently. Some of the existing approaches in vectorising seismograms are presented in the proprietary software and can only be achieved through restricted access. These methods have their advantages and shortcomings, and the use of these software so far is not at the level required for rapid and accurate processing of old analog seismograms. Reasons for this are a lack of fully developed open algorithms, finely adjusted to digitise specifically seismic data, and the fact that the geophysical digitising software for signals processing may not be available to through open access. 

In this study we used the DigitSeis software and demonstrated the successful approach of automated digitising seismograms using \ac{AI}. By employing methods DigitSeis software applies developed algorithms for the intelligent automated analyses of the seismograms considering the specific format and characteristics of these data (amplitude of traces, time gaps, 30-minute record intervals). The subsequent generation of digitised dataset on seismograms originally recorded in 1950s enabled to restore seismic information contained in historical records from Uccle station and analyse spectral characteristics of selected traces and parameters of ground motions. Another tested advantage of the DigitSeis consists in developed conversion to the .mat format for ease of use of generated file in the MATLAB environment to enable data integration and overlay of vectorised traces over the ole scanned images for data control. 

The demonstrated advantages of DigitSeis in digitising seismograms enable recurrent, and consistent information on seismic signal levels, repeatability of the recorded peaks, time gaps (or marks), period of data capture, intensity of signals, and strength of Earth's ground motion. All this information can be used for seismicity analysis and relevant geophysical studies. Using \ac{ML} approaches for digitising analog seismograms, DigitSeis presented a possibility for reuse the pool of seismic data accumulated since 1950s in Uccle station, Belgium. The demonstrated approach of DigitSeis implemented its algorithms on automated digitising of seismic traces and recognition of time gaps based on the threshold parameters.

Some functionality and operations in vectorising were limited and required manual corrections of recognised traces. This was performed by magnifying the fragments of the complicated cases and manual corrections (e.g. overlapped traces). Moreover, the technologies of DigitSeis still require improvements for processing of big data as streams. As a suggestion for this, we propose the combination of DigitSeis with Python for post-processing of vector data. However, general performance of DigitSeis shown reliable results in vectorisation of analog scanned seismograms.

Automated digitising of large seismogram datasets will enable to re-use archives of old seismograms as valuable source of information in computational geophysics while having an impact on development of earthquake prognosis using retrospective and perspective datasets in the regions with active seismicity. For this aim, copious amounts of old seismic data must be processed, classified, digitised and annotated for further integration and comparison of old and current seismic situation which would enable comparative analysis of the seismicity of the Earth. Once tested on several seismograms, the workflow can then be ported to larger volumes of data and cover seismology archive since 1950s up to now. 

Besides technical applications of practical methods of signal processing and vectorising by ML, this research falls within the scope of the geophysical and seismological research directions in historical data analysis of the Royal Observatory of Belgium (\cite{Camelbeeck2021}, \cite{Camelbeeck2020}, \cite{Camelbeecketal2019}, \cite{Lecocqetal2020}, \cite{VanCamp2017}, \cite{Stopa}). The vectorised archive of analog seismograms will be compiled in a digital historical library of the seismicity in Brabant Massif, Belgium, which will be fundamental to modelling geological hazard risks based on the retrospective data analysis. Therefore, the recommendation for future studies would be integration of several software and approaches for smooth data analysis in big volumes.
